3. What "vakri" really means: Mona and Minal in the park {{t:00:13:42}}

The speaker's core teaching example: a clock always ticks forward, yet we sometimes say our life feels like it is running backward. He uses two people walking in a park to show what a retrograde planet actually is. {{t:00:09:56}} {{t:00:13:42}}

The picture

Mona and Minal start walking together. Mona spots a cousin in the distance and races ahead. Minal, picking up something Mona dropped, keeps walking slowly in the same direction. {{t:00:13:42}} {{t:00:14:15}}

The illusion

Both are still walking forward the whole time. But because Mona is moving so much faster, from Mona's point of view Minal looks like she is falling behind, drifting backward, even though Minal never once reversed direction. {{t:00:14:46}} {{t:00:15:18}}

His definition

"Vakri" (retrograde) is exactly this look of going backward. A retrograde planet never truly reverses in space. It only looks that way against a faster-moving reference point such as the Earth. {{t:00:09:56}} {{t:00:15:50}}

  • Saturn takes about 30 years to go once around the Sun, Jupiter about 12. The Earth, going around in a single year, is far faster than either, so at certain points in the Earth's orbit, Jupiter or Saturn can appear to crawl backward against the stars. {{t:00:15:50}} {{t:00:16:23}}
  • His business example: one person earns a crore (10 million rupees) in two years, another takes five years for the same amount. People call the slower one "stuck", "retrograde", comparing him with the faster one, even though the slower person is still moving ahead in his own life. {{t:00:16:55}} {{t:00:17:27}}
  • He folds this back into the opening point: a person's own brain can run the same "vakri" trick, pulling attention backward into comparison with someone else's pace, rather than their own. {{t:00:17:27}} {{t:00:18:01}}

4. Why the appearance happens, and which planets ever go retrograde {{t:00:33:08}}

The speaker gives a second picture for the same idea, this time using the changing distance between the Earth and Jupiter, and ties it to a tea-strainer to introduce how remedies work. {{t:00:33:41}} {{t:00:34:45}}

  • As the Earth moves around the Sun, its distance to Jupiter keeps changing: his rough numbers go 100, then 90, then 92, then back past 100 to 110 kilometres (used loosely, as a stand-in for a changing distance, not an exact measurement). As the rays from Jupiter get closer, life speeds up; as they pull away, things that were going well slow down or reverse, "like tea you were about to swallow, spat back out". {{t:00:34:45}} {{t:00:36:17}}
  • If you were born while the sky-level transit of a planet happened to be retrograde, that placement is fixed as retrograde in your chart for life (written as "R" next to the planet). If it is not retrograde at your birth, a later sky-level retrograde period works against you instead of for you. {{t:00:36:40}} {{t:00:37:49}}
  • A sky-level retrograde period for any one planet is short, he says, often only a handful of weeks within a year, with the rest of the year spent moving forward (margi) again. {{t:00:37:49}}

Which planets go retrograde {{t:00:23:12}}

  • Never retrograde for us: the Sun and Moon, because, in his reasoning, both move faster around the Earth than the Earth itself does, so they cannot look like they are falling behind. {{t:00:23:12}} {{t:00:23:44}}
  • Sometimes retrograde: Mars, Mercury, Jupiter, Venus and Saturn, each on its own cycle. {{t:00:23:44}}
  • Always retrograde: Rahu and Ketu, by the speaker's reckoning, which is why their effects are treated differently from the other planets. {{t:00:23:44}}
  • He also places Uranus (which he calls Indra) and Neptune (Varun) among the slow outer bodies that can turn retrograde, giving rough orbital periods of about 84 years for Indra/Uranus and roughly 164 years for the Varun/Neptune body he is describing. {{t:01:59:49}}

The identification of Uranus with "Indra" and Neptune with "Varun" (and, elsewhere in the class, Pluto with "Yamraj") is the speaker's own naming scheme for the outer planets, not a classical Vedic designation, and is kept here only to follow his argument.
