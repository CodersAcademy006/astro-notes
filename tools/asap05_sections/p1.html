1. Mercury: a pipe with no character of its own {{t:00:00:33}}

Mercury (Budh) rules the sign Gemini (Mithun), the twins who face both ways: the same person can speak foolishly one minute and wisely the next. A famous story about two legendary Hindi playback singers opens the class: one was told his singing was useless by a music director {{t:00:01:35}} and sulked for a long time, and came back three years later a huge star {{t:00:02:38}}, at which point the same director praised the very same style of singing. The lesson the speaker draws: it was never about who was good or bad, it was the stretch of time, buddh and buddhiman (fool and wise one) are both faces of the same planet. {{t:00:03:40}}

  • Mercury sits at the throat. Its name literally means a door or channel (dwar). {{t:00:03:40}}
  • Mercury is, quite literally, your vani: your voice and your manner of speaking. {{t:00:01:03}}
  • A water pipe carries anything put into it, dirty water, clean water, coloured water, and keeps nothing of its own. The throat is the same: it carries truth and lies, good words and bad, and holds none of it back once it has passed. {{t:00:04:12}}
  • The throat has no gender and no morality built into it: not male, not female, not right, not wrong. Whatever goes in from the body comes out through it, and whatever is said, right or wrong, comes out the same way. {{t:00:04:12}}
  • If your Mercury is weak, people will not understand what you say even after you explain it again and again. If your Mercury is very strong, a person with less understanding can go mute in front of you and fail to argue back at all. {{t:00:05:46}}
  • A disagreement where "I don't understand what you mean" is really a Venus-vs-Mercury problem: Venus (which the speaker says governs rapport and bonding) is active between two people, while Mercury, which should carry the actual words, is being switched off. {{t:00:06:16}}

Mercury as the eunuch planet. The speaker's own term for Mercury's defining trait is napunsak (neuter, "without gender"): it does not take sides, is not male or female, good or bad.

  • Currency paper is Mercury's colour, green, and the speaker links the strength of a currency to how green its most powerful note is. {{t:00:07:19}}
  • Mercury carries wine, poison, nectar or food through the same channel without preference, "it has no stake in what passes through it." {{t:00:07:52}}
  • The speaker connects this to hijra (transgender) communities in his own tradition: he describes them as a human link to the neuter planet's blessing, since they neither marry in the ordinary sense nor have biological children, and for this reason are traditionally asked for blessings. He is clear this is his own reading of a social role, not a scientific claim about any individual. The speaker's own framework for a real, marginalised community; treat it as his personal view of a tradition, not a description of anyone's rights or worth. {{t:00:08:24}}
  • His broader point: expecting nothing from a relationship removes the possibility of hurt from it. Where there is no lena-dena (give and take expectation), there is no dukh (sorrow) either. {{t:00:08:55}}
  • His illustration: a stranger's insult on the street barely registers, but the same words from a neighbour or relative wound deeply, because attachment, not the words themselves, is what creates the hurt. {{t:00:09:26}}
  • On wanting too many things at once: a prayer that lists ten different wants in one breath rarely lands, in his telling, because the person has not actually settled on what they want; one clear, specific wish, which he links loosely to the "observer effect," is what he says can actually manifest. A personal blend of spiritual practice and a borrowed physics term; his own belief, not a scientific claim. {{t:00:52:09}}

Mercury has no fixed character of its own {{t:00:09:56}}

The speaker's picture: Mercury is the smallest, fastest planet, hugging the Sun so closely it never leaves its side, like a child who has not yet formed an independent mind.

  • A newborn is "ignorant": it becomes whatever is poured into it, a faith, an identity, a label, before it has any judgement of its own. The speaker gives this as a general description of early conditioning, not as a comment on any specific faith. His own framing of upbringing and belief; presented as his personal view. {{t:00:09:56}}
  • The Moon shapes early tendencies (sanskar from past life), Jupiter supplies the religion or framework you are raised in, and only later does a person's own buddhi (intellect, Mercury) develop and start to question what it was taught. {{t:00:10:27}}
  • A man's logic brain runs on Mercury: "this is right, that is wrong." The speaker contrasts this with a stereotype of women relying on intuition over logic in an argument; he frames it as his own generalisation for comic effect, not a rule. The speaker's own generalisation about men and women; not a factual claim. {{t:00:10:59}}

2. The birth story: Moon, Tara and why Mercury is green {{t:00:10:59}}

A traditional story, told as the speaker tells it, for why Mercury carries no fixed loyalty and why its colour is green.

Mercury travels so closely alongside the Sun that the speaker also calls the Sun its adoptive father in this telling. {{t:00:11:29}}

Jupiter (Brihaspati), king of the gods' teachers, is married to Tara. The Moon, restless and fickle by nature (the speaker notes it changes zodiac sign every two and a half to three days {{t:00:11:59}}), falls for Tara, and a child is born of that union: Mercury. On realising Jupiter is not his biological father, Mercury resents both parents, the Moon for an "illegitimate" birth and Jupiter for not truly being his father. Jupiter's response, in the story, is to forgive and embrace him as his own regardless: "I am older, I am his guardian, whatever my wife did happened, I love you anyway." {{t:00:12:29}}

Mercury's answer is different: "I don't care, I will use my own intellect; this is why the world keeps fighting, everyone insists their own god, their own faith is bigger." The speaker's point: without Jupiter's wisdom tempering it, raw intellect becomes constant one-upmanship and conflict, inside a single household as much as between nations. Four people in one home can each want a different vegetable for dinner because each is "using their own intellect" and no one will agree with anyone else. {{t:00:13:01}}

  • The sky's blue is really a trick of distance (we cannot see far enough into the vacuum for it to look black up close), and the speaker calls everything in that endless blue "Rahu." Whatever fills the near, bounded space within that blue, like the inside of a cup, he calls Mercury. {{t:00:04:43}}
  • His example for the cup: a cup is a visible, bounded container, so the empty space inside it is Mercury, while the vast, endless sky the cup sits within is Rahu. {{t:00:05:14}}
  • Jupiter's colour is yellow. Rahu's is blue. Mixed through the Moon's water (moisture, saliva, the fluid element in the body) the two make green, which the speaker gives as the real reason Mercury's colour is green and why green notes, green vegetation and green cloth are tied to this planet. {{t:00:14:33}}
  • A chandal yoga (an inauspicious pairing) between Jupiter and Rahu is traditionally seen as a problem, but the speaker reframes it: when Jupiter's wisdom and an awakened (witness-minded) Rahu combine well, "the chandal becomes a guru," and what comes out of your mouth is nectar rather than poison. His own reframing of a traditionally inauspicious yoga; a personal, not classical, reading. {{t:00:15:35}}
  • Money follows this logic too: if your Mercury is good, money comes, but money itself does not care how it was earned, "Lakshmi makes no distinction either," in the speaker's words. His own view on how money is earned and judged; not a claim that any method of earning is endorsed. {{t:00:07:52}}
  • What actually brings money, in his account, is not cleverness alone but firm resolve, "grab Krishna's finger," meaning commit fully to a course of action rather than earning through sheer intellect. {{t:00:17:09}}

3. Virgo, Durga and the Mahishasura story {{t:00:21:51}}

A traditional story the speaker uses to explain why the 6th sign, Virgo (Kanya), the sign Rahu rules best, is imagined as a young, unmarried girl riding a lion.

Death, tied to Pluto in his scheme, is said to arrive without warning and entirely in secret, unlike almost anything else in life, which is the backdrop to why a force capable of mastering death needed its own story. {{t:00:19:17}}

  • Virgo is sign number 6, owned by Rahu; Mercury's own sign is Gemini (Mithun), number 3. Each exalts best in the other's house: Mercury gives its best results in Virgo, Rahu gives its best results in Gemini. {{t:00:27:04}}
  • Virgo is classed as a two-legged, merchant-class (vaishya varna) sign in this scheme, spanning parts of three lunar mansions (Uttara Phalguni, all four padas of Hasta, and the first two padas of Chitra). {{t:00:27:37}}
  • The same symbol-matching logic, he says, explains the other zodiac images: an archer for Sagittarius, twin fish for Pisces, a water-bearer for Aquarius, each shape chosen to fit how that group of stars was once pictured. {{t:00:31:18}}
  • He adds a fertility-related aside: a woman, in his account, is born already carrying her lifetime supply of egg cells, while sperm keeps regenerating in a man, which he offers as part of his reasoning for why the first blessing in a new ritual year goes to an unmarried girl specifically. A biological claim about fertility presented as the speaker's own reasoning; not a medical statement, and not medical advice. {{t:00:32:54}}
  • On the number 108 (nakshatras times their four padas): the speaker also connects it to roughly how many solar or lunar diameters fit between Earth and the Sun, or Earth and the Moon, and treats it as part of the same reasoning for why 108 recurs in ritual counts. A numerological aside offered as his own reasoning; not an astronomical claim to rely on. {{t:00:26:33}}
  • Before the goddess appears, the speaker frames the gods' struggle against the demons as a war playing out inside the body's own ida and pingala energy channels, which he links to the aches and digestive troubles a person feels during stress. A personal, non-medical body framework; not a medical claim. {{t:00:19:49}}
  • He extends the demon imagery to biology: gut bacteria, in his telling, are a kind of necessary "demon," needed to break down food and waste even though too many of them cause illness, the same balance the goddess strikes by letting a small part of the slain demon survive. A loose, non-scientific analogy between mythology and gut bacteria; his own aside, not a biological claim. {{t:00:21:20}}
  • The demons in the story could not be killed by any god or any man, so the gods, in the telling, created a goddess instead: a kanya (unmarried girl, pre-childbearing), riding a lion (a symbol the speaker equates with the Sun, "the king" of the solar system), armed with every weapon, to defeat the buffalo-demon Mahishasura. A retelling of traditional mythology as the speaker frames it; presented as his own reading, not a historical or scientific claim. {{t:00:22:54}}
  • Mahishasura is not wholly destroyed in the story, only mostly, the way an antibiotic still needs some bacteria to remain in the body; the speaker's comparison is that a little bit of "demon" stays useful, kept in its place at the goddess's feet. {{t:00:23:25}}
  • Kanya (the unmarried girl, Virgo) is read as Mercury; a woman is Venus; a mother is the Moon. The speaker is careful to add that Virgo is a "female neuter" sign, unlike Saturn which he calls a "male neuter" planet: a man with a badly placed Saturn does not form sperm, while a woman with a badly placed Mercury, in his account, loses the capacity for sexual enjoyment or has trouble conceiving. The speaker's own claim about fertility and a specific planet; a personal view, not a medical statement, and not medical advice. {{t:00:25:30}}
  • This is why, in the Navratri festival he describes, the first worship on a new cycle goes to a young unmarried girl (kanjak/kanya puja) before anything else: an unmarried girl is said to hold the power of all the gods together and can "slay the buffalo" of accumulated faults. A description of a religious custom and the speaker's reasoning behind it; his own view, offered respectfully as tradition rather than fact. {{t:00:24:58}}
  • The buffalo itself, Mahishasura's form, is read as Pluto's vehicle and death's own symbol; the speaker's point is that Mercury (through the goddess imagined as Virgo) is specifically what can master that force, where ordinary strength cannot. A retelling of traditional mythology; the speaker's own reading, not a historical claim. {{t:00:28:39}}
  • In the same breath he adds that Venus is credited with sanjeevani, the mythical power to revive the dead, making Venus death's enemy in his scheme just as Mercury-as-Durga is death's conqueror in the Mahishasura story. His own reading of traditional mythology; not a factual claim. {{t:00:30:14}}
