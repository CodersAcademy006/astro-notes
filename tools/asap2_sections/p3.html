6. Your protective deity (Ishta): a shield you may not know you have {{t:00:38:34}}

Branching off the 9th-sign discussion, the speaker introduces "Ishta": a personal protective energy tied to your birth chart, present whether or not you know about it.

The claim

Everyone is born with both a protective energy (a "good" deity, linked to the Sun) and an opposing one (linked to Pluto); your chart's 9th sign decides which deity symbolically represents each. {{t:00:38:34}}

How it is found

By the 9th sign. His example: in the 6th sign, Durga is the protective Ishta and Venus sits debilitated there, so a person's protection may be Durga even if their devotion happens to be toward Shiva. {{t:00:39:36}}

What to actually do

Nothing is strictly required. The protection runs automatically. The one practical rule is to avoid your opposing deity's domain (he gives the example of avoiding a house of worship linked to an unfavourable Jupiter) rather than needing to actively worship your Ishta. {{t:00:40:39}}

  • Different religions, in his telling, grew out of different rulers and communities each formalising their own "cult" and customs (dress codes, dietary rules, purdah practices); he treats these as regional and historical, not cosmic requirements. {{t:01:15:40}}
  • He claims the universe works like a vast mirror: feelings sent outward through worship, in whatever religion's form, come back amplified; the specific deity's name or form does not change this mechanic. {{t:01:16:44}}
  • He encourages people without a known Ishta to simply trust whichever path works for them: a formless (nirakar) conception, as in Islam, Sikhism's Ek Onkar, or Christianity's "Son of God", are all, in his view, equally valid routes to the same underlying source energy. {{t:00:17:47}}
  • He adds that in some charts the Ishta is hard to determine or comes out weak, and such a person must have one deliberately "built" through a dedicated practice rather than relying on what the chart gives by default; he offers to cover this with worked chart examples in a future class. {{t:01:26:38}}
  • Wealth is not only Venus's to give. He claims Lakshmi (wealth) can arrive through several different graces: through Vishnu/the Sun, through Indra's sheer generosity, through Pluto in a way tied to religious or hidden means, or through Neptune in amounts so large he calls them "blinding"; which route opens, in his scheme, depends on which planet's grace a person is actually working with. {{t:00:46:54}}
  • His example for Neptune's grace: adoption. Krishna was raised by Yashoda, who loved him fully as her own without ever truly knowing his origin; he reads this willingness to embrace a child (or a parent) who "isn't technically yours" as Neptune's grace in action, open to anyone, not only to mythological figures. If your own parents are genuinely harmful, he suggests consciously adopting substitute parent figures and extending the same feeling toward them. {{t:00:48:29}}
  • The blood-transfusion analogy. He compares assuming one remedy or diet suits an entire family to assuming any blood type can be transfused into anyone: it simply does not work that way, since every chart's needs differ even between siblings. {{t:00:41:10}}
  • Once something is identified as personally negative for you, no amount of "hack" or workaround changes that: the only real tool is avoidance, the same way wearing glasses doesn't remove the need to avoid whatever is straining your eyes in the first place. {{t:00:45:49}}
  • He calls this deliberate practice of marking something as personally negative, then quietly filtering it out, "neuroplasticity": training your own mind to automatically avoid what harms you rather than policing every choice through guilt. {{t:00:43:45}}
  • He links this training to calming the body's two nervous-system branches (sympathetic and parasympathetic), framing it as activating what he calls the sushumna channel instead of staying stuck reacting through either extreme. {{t:00:44:18}}

What if someone is conditioned to treat their "negative" deity as a father figure? {{t:01:05:13}}

Asked what happens if a person has been raised to treat an unfavourable chart deity as a beloved father figure regardless, he turns the question back to fate itself: Lord Ram had three brothers, all sons of the same father, yet each had a different destiny. In his view, one deity cannot be assigned the same role for everyone in a family, since each person's own fortune (defined by their own chart) differs even among full siblings. {{t:01:05:46}}

The claims in this section, that a chart assigns a specific protective deity, and the broad comparisons between religions, are the speaker's personal spiritual framework. These notes present them as his opinion, not a factual claim about any religion, and do not endorse one faith over another.
